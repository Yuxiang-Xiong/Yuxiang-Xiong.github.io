Environmental measurement

More birds, or more birdwatchers?

A longer species list can reflect more watching. How can we recover what early visits missed?

Working paper · Research as of October 2026

The question

Two visits to the same wetland can produce different species lists. Some birds may have arrived, but others may simply have been missed on the first visit. The distinction matters when economists use bird records to measure environmental change.

What this study does

Using Chinese citizen bird records, the study keeps locations fixed and uses repeat sightings to model species presence separately from the chance of recording a species. It estimates the correction outside the cities used for validation.

In the validation exercise, the model sees only the earliest two visits. Corrected counts are compared with the fuller records accumulated through later visits.

What it finds

Average absolute error falls from 5.05 to 3.35 species, a reported reduction of 33.7%. The improvement remains under resampling and re-estimation. Simulations illustrate how accounting for the recording process can recover ecological associations obscured by observation changes.

Why it matters

Economic activity and policies can change both ecosystems and where people go birdwatching. Separating these processes helps researchers build more informative ecological measures.

Interpreting the evidence

The validation reference is the fuller later record, not a census of every species actually present. The correction assumes correct identification, sufficiently complete reporting, and approximately stable species presence during the short repeat-visit window. The percentage uses the manuscript's unrounded values.

The paper

Measuring Ecological Outcomes in Economics: Observation Bias and Repeat-Visit Correction in Chinese Citizen Bird Records

Abstract

Citizen-science records provide broad ecological coverage, but observation effort also affects measured outcomes. This study uses Chinese citizen bird records, fixed locations, and repeated visits to estimate species missed by limited observation. Validation compares corrected early-visit counts with fuller later records, with estimation conducted outside the cities being evaluated. Repeat-visit correction reduces average absolute error by 33.7%, with improvements retained under resampling and re-estimation. Simulations illustrate how separating species presence from recording can recover associations obscured by observation changes. Applications to mobility and pandemic risk show that measurement choices can materially change regression estimates. The study develops a practical sequence of record checks, spatial definition, correction, and validation for ecological measurement in economics.

Methods and data

  • China Bird Report records and fixed-location repeat visits.
  • Species-presence and recording models; held-out-city validation; resampling and simulations.
  • Citizen science
  • Biodiversity
  • Measurement error
