Family economics and care

How does childhood shape care later in life?

When parents develop health difficulties, adult children can respond through shared living, direct care, or financial support.

Working paper · Research as of October 2026

The question

How do childhood living arrangements relate to the ways adult children support parents after health difficulties arise? Moving into shared accommodation, providing direct care, and transferring money involve different commitments.

What this study does

The study links childhood histories to adult child-parent household records in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. It tracks the onset of parental functional difficulties and compares changes in shared living, direct care, and material support.

The updated analysis checks residence nonresponse, sample selection, fixed-start follow-up, and comparisons within families.

What it finds

In the corrected adjacent-transition sample, childhood separation is associated with a weaker entry-into-shared-living response. Direct care increases after parental difficulties among children with separation histories, but the difference in care responses between groups is imprecisely estimated.

Fixed-start and within-family comparisons do not establish a consistent long-run shared-living difference. Financial and in-kind support also do not show a stable compensating pattern.

Why it matters

Family support cannot be summarized by one outcome. Studying several forms of help connects early family experiences with the practical choices households face when care needs arise.

Interpreting the evidence

The shared-living result depends on sample selection and specification. Entering co-residence does not reveal which family member moved. The evidence does not establish that childhood separation causally weakens all forms of eldercare, or that direct care substitutes for shared living.

The paper

Childhood Parental Absence and Eldercare Support under Parental Health Risks

Abstract

This project studies how childhood parental co-residence relates to adult children's support responses when parents develop functional difficulties. Linked life-history and household panel records distinguish entry into shared living, direct care, and material transfers. Updated comparisons correcting residence nonresponse show a weaker shared-living response among children with childhood separation in a selected adjacent-transition sample. Direct care increases after parental difficulties, while group differences in that response are imprecise. Fixed-start follow-up and within-family comparisons do not establish a consistent long-run shared-living effect, and material-support patterns vary. The project treats different forms of family support separately and retains the role of selection, measurement, and specification in interpreting the evidence.

Methods and data

  • China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study life-history and child-parent household panel records.
  • Adjacent-transition and fixed-start comparisons; pair and family comparisons; residence-measurement and sample-selection checks.
