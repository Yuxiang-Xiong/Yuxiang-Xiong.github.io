Urban and regional economics

When does a major project become local development?

Bringing a factory to a place is one step. Connecting it to local firms, workers, and services is another.

Working paper · Research as of October 2026

The question

A city can bring investment and expertise to another place, yet still struggle to coordinate land, infrastructure, approvals, and construction. How do governance arrangements help projects become operational, and how does project growth relate to wider local development?

What this study does

The study examines the Shenshan Special Cooperation Zone around the 2017 shift toward Shenzhen-led governance. It combines synthetic-control comparisons, local spatial grids, land-transfer records, economic-census information, and public accounts of project implementation.

What it finds

Built-up space and some infrastructure-related land supply expand. Major industrial projects subsequently reach large-scale production, with industrial employment growth concentrated in large establishments.

Construction and commercial locations also grow beyond the immediate project surroundings, but their patterns differ from industrial production. Absolute local growth and performance relative to nearby areas are not the same comparison.

Why it matters

The study connects the organization of project delivery with the geographic reach of growth. Coordinating development tasks can help investment become operational; sustained links with local businesses, workers, and services matter for broader development.

Interpreting the evidence

Some estimates vary with measurement, comparison areas, and spatial inference, and some market indicators show pre-existing trends. The project does not equate construction or industrial output with a demonstrated improvement in residents' welfare.

The paper

From Project Growth to Regional Development: Evidence from the Shenshan Special Cooperation Zone

Abstract

This paper studies how cross-regional cooperation turns external resources into local project-delivery capacity and regional development. Using the Shenshan Special Cooperation Zone's 2017 governance adjustment, it combines synthetic controls, local grid comparisons, land-transfer records, economic-census information, and public project histories. The evidence shows relative built-up expansion and increased transport and public-facility land supply, followed by large-scale production in major industrial projects. Industrial employment growth is concentrated in larger establishments. Construction and commercial locations also expand outside project surroundings, with patterns distinct from industrial production. The paper connects administrative coordination and project implementation with the distribution of local growth, while distinguishing project scale from wider regional development.

Methods and data

  • Synthetic controls; local grid and town comparisons; land-transfer records.
  • Economic-census information; built-up area, night-time lights and commercial locations; institutional and project histories.
  • Regional governance
  • Spatial development
  • Shenshan
