International trade and ecology

When trade changes, what happens to birds?

Economic adjustment can reshape local landscapes. Bird monitoring offers another way to study those changes.

Working paper · Research as of October 2026

The question

Trade changes can alter employment, production, and land use. Does exposure to China's receipt of Permanent Normal Trade Relations also appear in repeated bird surveys in the United States?

What this study does

The study links county trade-policy exposure to repeated breeding-bird surveys. It compares more- and less-exposed counties before and after the policy, examining species richness, ecological groups, and the persistence of newly recorded species.

What it finds

The overall association with species richness is small and statistically uncertain. Point estimates are more positive on routes with long monitoring histories and for migratory rather than resident species.

Tracking entries, disappearances, and later sightings distinguishes a change in the length of a species list from a change in the continuity of the local community.

Why it matters

A single species count can conceal changes in which birds are present and whether they remain. Bringing species identity and persistence into the analysis gives a fuller ecological perspective on economic adjustment.

Interpreting the evidence

The findings do not establish that trade improves biodiversity overall. Monitoring history, species composition, and statistical uncertainty are part of the result, not details to be set aside.

The paper

Trade Policy Exposure and Bird Species Richness: Evidence from United States Breeding Bird Surveys

Abstract

This study links United States exposure to China's receipt of Permanent Normal Trade Relations to repeated breeding-bird surveys. It examines species richness, ecological groups, and the persistence of newly recorded species. Comparisons of more- and less-exposed counties before and after the policy yield a small, statistically uncertain overall richness association. Point estimates are more positive on long-monitored routes and for migratory rather than resident species. Tracking entries, disappearances, and subsequent records distinguishes changes in species counts from the continuity of local communities. The study connects trade-related economic adjustment with species identity and repeated ecological occurrence.

Methods and data

  • United States Breeding Bird Survey records; county exposure to Permanent Normal Trade Relations.
  • Continuous-exposure before-and-after comparisons; monitoring-history checks; species-group and persistence analysis.
  • International trade
  • Biodiversity
  • Species persistence
