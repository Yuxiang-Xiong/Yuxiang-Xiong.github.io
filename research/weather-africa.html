Climate adaptation and information

Can weather information limit disaster losses?

Rainfall shocks do not translate into the same human losses everywhere. Local information capacity may be part of the explanation.

Working paper · Research as of September 2026

The question

Why can similar weather shocks have different human consequences across places? This project studies whether local weather-station coverage is associated with the losses recorded after rainfall shocks in Africa.

What this study does

The project combines rainfall measures, histories of active weather stations, and geographically located disaster records. It compares local areas while accounting for broader regional and time differences.

It also checks how the findings depend on rainfall products, the assignment of event-level deaths to local areas, and the countries and events that actually support estimation.

What it finds

The earlier district-level analysis finds smaller increases in constructed death losses near active stations after rainfall deficits. Later flood-focused checks show that the precision and size of the station association vary with the rainfall measure, geographic allocation, and controls.

These analyses concern different weather comparisons and should not be read as a single, uniform estimate of lives saved.

Why it matters

Weather information is one part of adaptation capacity. Understanding its relationship with losses requires examining both the information network and how disasters are reported and located.

Interpreting the evidence

Station locations are not randomly assigned, and reported event deaths must be allocated to local areas. Some estimates rely on a small effective set of countries and events. The evidence does not identify the causal effect of building a weather station, and warning receipt or evacuation timing is not directly measured.

The paper

Weather Information and Disaster Death Losses in Africa

Abstract

This project examines the relationship between local weather information capacity and disaster death losses in Africa using rainfall data, weather-station histories, and geocoded disaster records. Earlier district-level comparisons associate proximity to active stations with smaller increases in constructed death losses after rainfall deficits. Subsequent flood-focused diagnostics show sensitivity to rainfall measures, spatial allocation of losses, controls, and effective sample support. Recording a disaster and measuring its severity are distinct outcomes. The project studies observational relationships between information infrastructure and losses rather than treating station coverage as a randomly assigned intervention.

Methods and data

  • District-level rainfall; active weather-station histories; geocoded disaster records.
  • Local panel comparisons; alternative rainfall and loss-allocation measures; spatial and effective-sample diagnostics.
  • Climate adaptation
  • Disaster risk
  • Africa
